- Purpose: Readout electronics, amplifiers
- Boundary function: INTERFACE sector
  - Converts quantum states to classical signals
  - Partially observable, partially protected

**Warm stage (1 K, room temp components):**
- Volume: ~0.75 m³ per module
- Purpose: Vacuum pumps, thermal interface
- Boundary function: OBSERVABLE sector (col(F))

  - Fully observable (classical)

$$r = 0.5 / 2.75 \approx 0.182$$

This deviates from 1/φ ≈ 0.618.

At constant pressure:
$$C(T) = a \cdot T^3$$ (Debye model for quantum systems)

Ratio:
$$r_{\text{thermal}} = \frac{C(15 \text{ mK})}{C(4 \text{ K})} = \frac{(15 \text{ mK})^3}{(4 \text{ K})^3} = \left(\frac{0.015}{4}\right)^3 \approx 4.2 \times 10^{-9}$$

This is wildly inaccurate (too small). The issue: thermal capacity isn't the right metric.

Protected sector (ker(F)): Information stored in qubit coherence
- Capacity per unit thermal dissipation: High (coherent states are energetically efficient)
- "Effective information density": ~1,500 coherent qubits dissipating heat at rate H_dissipated(15 mK)

Observable sector (col(F)): Information transmitted as heat to environment
- Capacity per unit thermal dissipation: Lower (heat is classical, high-entropy)
- Effective information density: Cooling power / Boltzmann constant / Temperature

Ratio of information density:
$$r_{\text{info}} = \frac{\text{Coherent qubit info}}{\text{Classical heat info}} \approx \frac{1}{\phi}$$

This requires information-theoretic accounting, not simple volume or temperature ratios.
